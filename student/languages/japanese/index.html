Lesson 1 — Hiragana: first writing system

Japanese uses Hiragana, Katakana and Kanji together. Hiragana is usually the first script taught to beginners; Katakana is commonly used for many loanwords and emphasis, while Kanji represents characters used in words and grammatical contexts.

Writing routine

  1. Look at one kana and its stroke order.
  2. Trace it, then write it from memory.
  3. Read it aloud using audio or a trusted pronunciation reference.
  4. Mix the characters and test recognition without the Romaji prompt.
Romanisation: Standard Hepburn is used as a beginner reading aid here. Learn kana directly and avoid depending on Romaji for long-term reading.

Lesson 2 — Pronunciation, spelling and rhythm

  • Japanese rhythm is mora-based. Count timing units, not only English-style syllables.
  • Long vowels can change word meaning; write and hear the difference carefully.
  • The small っ (sokuon) indicates a brief consonant hold/doubling in many words.
  • ん is a moraic nasal; its realisation varies with the following sound.
  • Learn voiced marks (が, ざ, だ, ば) and semi-voiced marks (ぱ).
  • Learn contracted sounds such as きゃ, しゅ, ちょ after the basic kana set.

For listening, compare short vs long vowels and words with vs without っ. Use reviewed audio rather than assuming that Romaji fully communicates timing.

Note: the particle は is written “ha” but pronounced “wa” when used as the topic marker. This is a spelling/pronunciation distinction learners should practise early.

Lesson 4 — Sentence structure and particles

Japanese commonly places the verb at the end of the sentence. Particles follow nouns or phrases to indicate topic, subject, object, location, direction and other functions.

PatternExampleবাংলা ব্যাখ্যা
Topic はわたしは学生です。আমি শিক্ষার্থী।
Object を + verb本を読みます。বই পড়ি।
Location で + action学校で勉強します。স্কুলে পড়াশোনা করি।
Question か学生ですか。আপনি কি শিক্ষার্থী?

These are foundation patterns. Politeness, omitted subjects, particles, verb forms and context require continued study.
