WHAT “INTELLIGENCE” MEANS HERE

Not just news. Not just links. A decision you can explain.

FinTech intelligence turns relevant market, technology, customer, operational and regulatory signals into a structured view of an opportunity or risk. The output is not a prediction or a compliance certificate. It is a documented decision brief: what changed, why it matters, what evidence is missing, what options exist and what to do next.

01

Detect a signal

A product trend, customer complaint, fraud pattern, technology dependency, official notice or market change worth investigating.

02

Interpret business impact

Connect the signal to a real question: launch, pricing, onboarding, control design, vendor choice, investment or expansion.

03

Check evidence

Separate verified facts from assumptions; identify the official source, date, jurisdiction, data needed and unanswered questions.

04

Make the next move

Compare options, name an accountable owner, define a test or control, and set a review date. A human decision-maker remains responsible.

The practical output: a short intelligence brief, evidence checklist, decision options and action plan—not a pile of links or an unexplained spreadsheet.

WHO CAN USE IT

Different users. Different decisions.

Use the hub to frame a question before opening a specialised tool. The value is in choosing the right evidence and translating it into a decision relevant to your role.

FinTech founders & entrepreneurs

Assess a product idea, target market, partner dependency, customer trust issue or expansion plan before committing significant time and capital.

  • What needs validating before launch?
  • Which partners, risks and controls need due diligence?
  • What is the smallest safe pilot?

Banks & financial institutions

Translate emerging technology and risk signals into questions for product, compliance, operations, risk and internal audit teams.

  • What control evidence should be requested?
  • Who owns remediation and retesting?
  • What must be escalated for formal review?

Payment providers & merchants

Explore payment flow, onboarding, disputes, fraud patterns, cross-border dependencies and service continuity.

  • Where can a transaction fail or be abused?
  • What should be monitored in a pilot?
  • Which exceptions need human review?

Investors, consultants & product teams

Structure early due diligence and compare business-model assumptions without treating a desk review as proof.

  • Which claims require independent evidence?
  • What dependencies could change the business case?
  • What evidence should be requested next?

FREE GLOBAL WORK PRODUCT · REUSABLE FIELD KIT

FinTech Inclusion Impact Canvas

A practical worksheet for founders, banks, NGOs, researchers, product teams and students to test whether a digital financial service is genuinely accessible, affordable, safe and useful for people who are often left out. Use it for any country or market—then export a working action register.

Designed for real-world usefulness

Move beyond counting app downloads. Examine the full customer journey, barriers, evidence and remedies—from first contact to complaints and exit.

Open and reusable

Free to use and adapt with attribution to RegTech Nexus AI. This is a structured self-review, not a certification, ranking or proof of impact.

Privacy by design

Your entries stay in this browser session. This worksheet does not submit them to RegTech Nexus AI. Do not enter personal, account or confidential customer data.

For each dimension, choose the status supported by your current evidence and add a source, observation or question. If you select “Gap identified” or “Not assessed”, the generated action register will flag follow-up work.

01Reach and practical access

Can intended users reach and use the service with their actual device, connectivity, electricity, location and access to cash-in/cash-out or support?

02Affordability and transparency

Are all fees, data costs, minimum balances, penalties, exchange rates and likely indirect costs understandable and manageable for the target group?

03Language, literacy and accessibility

Can people with different languages, reading levels, disabilities and digital skills complete key tasks independently, including recovery from errors?

04Fair and workable onboarding

Are identity, documentation and verification requirements risk-based, lawful and practical for people who may lack standard documents—without bypassing required safeguards?

05Trust, privacy and informed choice

Do users understand consent, data use, permissions, sharing, automated decisions and how to stop or change the service?

06Fraud prevention and safe recovery

Can users recognise scams, report unauthorised activity, secure or recover an account and obtain timely help without being unfairly blamed?

07Complaints and customer redress

Can customers raise a complaint in an accessible way, track it, receive a clear decision and challenge an error or transaction outcome?

08Fair treatment and outcome gaps

Are approval, pricing, fraud flags, service quality and drop-off reviewed across relevant user groups for unjustified disparities and unintended harm?

09Reliability and continuity

What happens during outages, weak connectivity, failed transfers, delayed settlements or agent downtime—and how are users informed and made whole?

10Measurable benefit—not just sign-ups

Will you measure whether the service improves a real outcome, such as safer saving, lower total cost, time saved, reliable payment or business resilience?

Start by describing the service and reviewing each dimension. No numerical score is produced: a single score can hide serious barriers for a particular group.

MORE PRACTICAL SCENARIOS

What a user can actually do with this hub

Each example starts with a business problem and ends with a useful work product. The existing tools support parts of the analysis; they do not automatically perform every step listed.

01Digital lender assessing a new customer segment

Question: Should the lender pilot small-ticket credit for a new customer group?

Analyse: Customer need and affordability assumptions; data quality and consent; model limitations; fair treatment; fraud, complaints and collections controls; applicable official requirements.

Work product: Assumption register, evidence checklist, model/use-case review questions, pilot limits and monitoring indicators.

Next step: Use Bank-use Review and Explainable AI resources for governance questions; use relevant regulatory sources and professional review for jurisdiction-specific obligations.

02Payment provider investigating rising disputes

Question: Are disputes associated with a merchant category, onboarding weakness, service outage or confusing customer journey?

Analyse: Segment disputes by time, merchant, channel and reason; validate data quality; compare process changes; inspect exception handling.

Work product: A hypothesis log, evidence request list, control-owner map and time-bound test plan.

Next step: Validate with authorised internal data and the organisation's approved systems. This public hub does not connect to payment rails or private customer records.

03Bank evaluating an AI vendor

Question: Is the proposed AI tool suitable for a defined banking use case?

Analyse: Intended use, data access, explainability, human override, vendor dependency, security evidence, validation, incident handling and exit arrangements.

Work product: Due-diligence questions, evidence-gap list, accountable owners and conditions before production use.

Next step: Open Bank-use Review and Explainable AI controls. Do not treat a completed checklist as approval or certification.

04Trade FinTech reviewing a suspicious-looking transaction pattern

Question: Which trade, party, route or document inconsistencies merit closer review?

Analyse: Compare the transaction story with available invoices, transport documents, goods, counterparties and payment terms; distinguish anomalies from evidence of misconduct.

Work product: A documented review trail, missing-document list, reason for escalation and decision owner.

Next step: Use TradeGuard with authorised, non-sensitive or synthetic examples and follow the institution's escalation process.

05Entrepreneur comparing two markets

Question: Which market should be researched first for a digital wallet or embedded-finance product?

Analyse: Customer problem, competition, payment infrastructure, partner availability, official regulatory pathway, operating costs, data protection and consumer recourse.

Work product: A comparable market scorecard with sourced facts, explicit assumptions, evidence gaps and a shortlist for deeper diligence.

Next step: Start with official jurisdiction sources and relevant Regulatory Signal Labs; do not rank markets on incomplete or unverified data.

06Compliance team converting a policy into actions

Question: Does a policy describe the control clearly enough for staff to perform and auditors to test it?

Analyse: Control objective, trigger, owner, procedure, evidence, exception path, reporting and review frequency.

Work product: Policy-to-control mapping, missing-evidence list and remediation tracker.

Next step: Use Policy Gap Analyzer for the relevant comparison and ICMS Toolkit to organise owners, due dates and retesting.

A REUSABLE WORK PRODUCT

What a FinTech Intelligence Brief should contain

Use this structure for a product review, market scan, vendor assessment or risk question. It makes the reasoning inspectable instead of presenting a long list of webpages or a spreadsheet without context.

PRACTICAL WORKING TEMPLATERECORD EVIDENCE & OPEN QUESTIONS

FinTech Intelligence Brief

1. Decision to make
What choice must the team make, by when, and who approves it?
2. Signal and context
What changed? Include date, jurisdiction, product, source and why it matters.
3. Verified evidence
List primary sources and evidence reviewed; note scope, status and last checked date.
4. Facts vs assumptions
Separate established facts, working hypotheses and unknowns.
5. Impact and options
Describe potential customer, commercial, operational, technology and control impacts; compare options.
6. Gaps and limitations
State missing evidence, uncertainty, out-of-scope questions and what was not tested.
7. Action plan
Action, accountable owner, due date, evidence required, escalation path and retest date.
8. Decision record
Proceed / revise / pause / reject, with rationale and approval through the appropriate process.

How the hub helps

  1. Use this page to define the decision.
  2. Open the specialist module for the relevant part of the analysis.
  3. Return to the brief to record verified findings and gaps.
  4. Have the responsible expert or decision-maker review it.
  5. Track actions and retest through approved internal processes.

Make a spreadsheet actionable: for each useful source, record why it matters, the jurisdiction, publication date, verification status, the question it helps answer and the next action. A large URL list without this context is only an inventory—not a decision-ready analysis.

A REPEATABLE ANALYSIS METHOD

From signal to defensible action

The same evidence discipline can support many FinTech questions without forcing every problem into one scoring model.

  1. 01
    Frame the question

    Define the product, transaction, institution, jurisdiction and decision that need analysis.

  2. 02
    Verify authoritative sources

    Check the primary source, date, legal status, scope and amendments. International standards are not automatically domestic law.

  3. 03
    Select the dedicated tool

    Use the relevant existing module; do not treat one tool's output as another tool's result.

  4. 04
    Inspect evidence and limitations

    Record what is known, missing, assumed or not verified. Risk indicators are not proof of wrongdoing.

  5. 05
    Assign action and retest

    Where appropriate, record the owner, due date, supporting evidence, approval and retest result in the institution's approved process.

PLAIN-LANGUAGE GUIDE

FinTech terms, explained simply

You do not need to know every acronym before you begin. Use these short definitions as a starting point, then check the relevant official source for the context that applies to your work.

AML/CFT
Anti-money laundering and countering the financing of terrorism: laws, processes and controls intended to prevent and detect the misuse of financial services.
KYC
Know Your Customer: identifying and understanding a customer, including the checks required for the relevant service and risk.
TBML
Trade-based money laundering: using trade transactions, documents or value differences to disguise the movement of illicit funds.
RegTech
Technology that helps organisations manage regulatory or compliance-related processes, evidence and obligations.
SupTech
Supervisory technology: technology used by regulators or supervisors to support oversight, analysis and supervision.
Model governance
The oversight around how a model is designed, tested, approved, monitored, changed and retired—including its limitations and accountability.
Regulatory readiness
A structured review of what a business may need to understand, document or arrange before carrying out a regulated activity. It is not a licence or a compliance guarantee.
Primary source
An official document or publication from the authority or organisation responsible for issuing it. Check the date, jurisdiction, status and scope.
Control evidence
Records that help show whether a control was designed, performed and reviewed—for example, an approved procedure, test result or exception record.
Human review
A qualified person checks context, evidence and limitations before an important decision is made; an automated indicator alone is not proof.
