DERIVATIVES · RISK · REGULATION · COMPLIANCE

Derivatives through a RegTech lens.

Financial derivatives connect markets, treasury, risk, accounting, governance and regulatory controls. This page provides a structured reference for understanding forwards, futures, options, swaps and related exposures through risk, compliance, reporting and audit-readiness perspectives.

Explore regulatory landscape ↓View control framework
01 · FUNDAMENTALS

What are derivatives?

A derivative is a financial contract whose value changes in response to an underlying asset, rate, index, price, credit characteristic or other reference factor. Derivatives can support legitimate hedging and risk-management objectives, but can also introduce or transfer significant risks.

Forwards

Contracts to transact at specified future terms. Key considerations include counterparty exposure, market movement and documentation.

Futures

Standardised exchange-traded contracts. Margin, leverage, market risk and clearing arrangements are important considerations.

Options

Contracts providing a right, generally without an obligation, to transact under specified terms. Premium, volatility and valuation are key considerations.

Swaps

Contracts exchanging specified cash flows, including interest-rate and currency exposures. Valuation, counterparty and liquidity risks matter.

Credit derivatives

Instruments used to transfer or manage credit exposure, subject to applicable regulatory, legal and risk requirements.

OTC vs exchange-traded

OTC transactions may require additional attention to bilateral documentation, collateral, reporting, netting and counterparty risk.

02 · INSTITUTIONAL USE

Why banks and businesses use derivatives

Hedging

  • Foreign exchange risk
  • Interest-rate risk
  • Commodity-price risk
  • Certain credit exposures

Treasury management

Derivatives may be used to manage funding costs, currency exposure, interest-rate exposure and other balance-sheet risks.

Risk transfer

A derivative can transfer or redistribute an economic risk between counterparties. A reduction in one risk can introduce another.

03 · RISK MAP

Key derivatives risks

Market risk

Changes in interest rates, FX rates, commodity prices, equity prices, volatility or other market factors can change derivative values.

Counterparty credit risk

A counterparty may default while the transaction has positive value. Exposure measurement, limits, netting and collateral are key controls.

CVA risk

Changes in counterparty credit quality and relevant market factors can affect credit valuation adjustments.

Liquidity risk

Margin calls, collateral requirements, funding pressure and difficulty exiting positions can create liquidity stress.

Operational risk

Trade capture, lifecycle processing, settlement, reconciliation and data-quality failures can create material exposure.

Legal & model risk

Contract enforceability, netting, collateral terms, pricing models, assumptions and market data require effective governance.

04 · REGULATORY LANDSCAPE

Derivatives regulation by jurisdiction

Requirements vary by jurisdiction, product, counterparty, trading venue, clearing status and transaction type. Always verify current primary-source requirements before operational use.

Bangladesh

Bangladesh Bank's derivatives guidance addresses permitted derivatives, underlying exposure, hedging, risk management, suitability/appropriateness, documentation, reporting and approval conditions. The framework is particularly relevant to AD banks and eligible users.

Bangladesh Bank Derivatives Guideline ↗

Basel / BCBS

Relevant Basel areas include counterparty credit risk, SA-CCR, CVA risk, market risk, capital requirements and related disclosure and risk-management expectations.

Basel Framework ↗

European Union

EMIR addresses OTC derivatives, central counterparties and trade repositories, including reporting, clearing and risk-mitigation requirements. MiFID II/MiFIR may also be relevant depending on the activity.

European Commission — EMIR ↗

United States

The US framework includes Dodd-Frank reforms and CFTC requirements covering areas such as swap reporting, clearing, execution, business conduct, record keeping and risk management, subject to applicability.

CFTC — Dodd-Frank ↗
Regulatory boundary: A framework listed here does not mean every requirement applies to every institution or transaction. Applicability must be determined from the current primary source, entity status, product, jurisdiction and transaction facts.

06 · MARGIN & COLLATERAL

Margin and collateral management

Initial Margin

Designed to address potential future exposure over the relevant close-out period under the applicable framework.

Variation Margin

Generally reflects changes in current transaction or portfolio value and the applicable collateral arrangements.

Collateral controls

Eligibility, valuation, haircuts, margin calls, disputes, settlement, reconciliation and concentration monitoring may require dedicated controls.

07 · IFRS 9

Derivatives and accounting

Derivatives may have important accounting implications, including fair-value measurement and hedge-accounting requirements. Relevant areas include FVTPL, cash-flow hedges, fair-value hedges, documentation, effectiveness and disclosure. Accounting treatment should be determined under the applicable accounting framework and approved accounting policies.

08 · FINANCIAL CRIME

AML/CFT and sanctions considerations

Derivatives are not inherently suspicious. Institutions should assess whether activity is consistent with the customer's business, risk profile and underlying economic rationale.

Potential review indicators

  • Unexplained complex structures
  • Unusual counterparties
  • Rapid or repeated termination/rebooking
  • Unusual collateral movements
  • Unexplained economic exposure
  • Potential market-manipulation patterns

Control chain

KYC/CDD → Beneficial Ownership → Sanctions Screening → Transaction Monitoring → Counterparty Risk → Escalation → Investigation → Record Keeping

Red-flag boundary

A red flag is an indicator requiring further review. It is not, by itself, proof of money laundering, sanctions evasion, market abuse or other misconduct.

12 · PRACTICAL CASES

From exposure to evidence

FX exposure

Commercial exposure → FX risk → permitted hedge → trade → valuation → monitoring → reporting → evidence.

Interest-rate exposure

Floating-rate liability → interest-rate risk → appropriate derivative → counterparty assessment → valuation → monitoring.

Commodity exposure

Import exposure → commodity-price risk → eligible hedge → underlying-document verification → margin/collateral → monitoring.

Control failure

Incorrect trade capture → incorrect exposure → reporting discrepancy → reconciliation exception → investigation → remediation → retest.
